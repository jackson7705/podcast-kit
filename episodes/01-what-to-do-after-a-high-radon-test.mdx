If your radon test came back high, do not panic and do not ignore it. Confirm the result with a follow-up test, arrange mitigation if the confirmed level calls for it, and verify the result after the system is running.

<!--hook-->

Radon is a long-term exposure risk. One elevated reading does not mean you need to leave your house tonight. It means you have a clear sequence to follow, and the first step is confirming the number.

A short-term reading reflects the weather, the season, and how the house was closed during that particular test. If the first result was between four and ten picocuries per liter, the Environmental Protection Agency recommends a second short-term test or a long-term test. If it was ten or higher, use another short-term test so you can confirm it sooner.

When you use two short-term tests, average the results. If that average is four picocuries per liter or higher, the agency recommends mitigation. Run the follow-up test on the lowest level of the home that you regularly use. Keep windows and exterior doors closed during a short-term test, except for normal entry and exit.

Once the elevated level is confirmed, a qualified contractor can assess the foundation, choose suitable suction points, and design the system around the home. In many St. Louis homes, the solution is active sub-slab depressurization. A pipe and fan draw soil gas from beneath the foundation and vent it safely above the roofline.

Installation for a typical home is usually completed in one day. The system starts lowering indoor radon within about twenty-four hours, but a running fan does not finish the job. A post-mitigation test is what confirms the result.

That is the whole path: confirm the reading, install the right system when needed, and test again. A high result is serious, but it is also a problem with a well-established solution.
