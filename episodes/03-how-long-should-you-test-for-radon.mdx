For a fast radon reading, test for two to seven days. For the best picture of your home's year-round average, test for more than ninety days.

<!--hook-->

The right testing window depends on the decision you need to make. A short-term test answers quickly. A long-term test smooths out the changes caused by weather, seasons, and the way the home is used.

Every radon test should run for at least forty-eight hours. For a short-term test lasting fewer than four days, the Environmental Protection Agency advises keeping the house closed for at least twelve hours before the test begins. Keep windows and exterior doors closed during the test, except for normal entry and exit.

Place the device on the lowest level of the home that is regularly used. Keep it away from drafts, exterior walls, and high humidity. These conditions matter because extra ventilation can make the reading look lower than the level the home normally carries.

A short-term test is usually the practical choice when you need an answer soon. That includes a first screening and most real estate transactions. A home purchase rarely leaves enough time for a test that runs more than three months.

A long-term test is the better choice when there is no deadline and you want the most representative number. Radon rises and falls. A measurement that spans more than ninety days averages more of those swings instead of capturing one particular week.

If a short-term result is four picocuries per liter or higher, confirm it with a follow-up test before deciding on mitigation. A second result helps distinguish a persistent level from a temporary spike. Homes should also be retested periodically, including after major renovations or changes to the heating and ventilation system.

So choose the test length to match the question. Use a short-term test when speed matters. Use a long-term test when the year-round average matters most. In either case, careful placement and proper testing conditions are what make the number useful.
